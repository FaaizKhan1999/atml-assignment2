\begin{abstract}
  Post-training adapts pretrained language models to preferences and task constraints, but training objectives and feedback sources directly influence response length, refusal calibration, and optimization stability. We evaluate three preference optimization methods (DPO, PPO, GRPO) and two feedback sources (exact outcome verification and AI judgment) on \texttt{Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct} with LoRA adapters. Specifically: (i) training DPO on length-balanced pairs widens the accuracy gap between rejected-longer and preferred-longer completions from 17.1\% to 31.1\% ($N = 237$), favoring shorter responses; (ii) on cached rollouts, tight PPO clipping ($\epsilon = 0.05$) bounds 16.0\% of tokens, but shows higher loss and gradient variance during continuation; (iii) GRPO informativeness reaches 100\% on easy prompts but plateaus at 91.7\% on hard prompts (11 of 12 groups informative); (iv) on XSTest, the 3B automated judge reported zero safe over-refusals across all four policies, whereas a 240-item audit across 60 prompts (72.1\% agreement, 173/240) revealed that all four policies refused the same 10 benign prompts, behavior inherited directly from SFT; and (v) on controlled math perturbations, deterministic verifiers detected all outcome errors ($S_{\text{outcome}} = 1.0$) but were invariant to reasoning corruption, whereas the pairwise AI judge tied on all 20 outcome errors and favored persuasive filler in 25.0\% of pairs. These findings show that length balancing shifts accuracy across length strata rather than eliminating length sensitivity, while our automated judge masked policy over-refusal and proved vulnerable to persuasive framing. Code: \url{https://github.com/FaaizKhan1999/atml-assignment2}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Modern language model development commonly follows a three-stage pipeline: pretraining learns general text representations, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) teaches models to follow instructions, and post-training aligns outputs with preferences or reward signals \citep{ouyang2022instructgpt}. Common post-training methods include Direct Preference Optimization \citep[DPO;][]{rafailov2023dpo}, Proximal Policy Optimization \citep[PPO;][]{schulman2017ppo, ouyang2022instructgpt}, and Group Relative Policy Optimization \citep[GRPO;][]{shao2024deepseekmath}. Unlike pretraining and SFT, which train models to predict or imitate text distributions, post-training optimizes a scalar or pairwise objective directly. This shift from imitation to reward maximization makes the policy vulnerable to optimizing proxies rather than desired behaviors, making it essential to analyze the failure modes of this stage.

However, optimizing an objective does not ensure that the policy meets the intended goal. Standard evaluation metrics like reward scores, benchmark accuracy, and reference KL divergence each measure only a single property in isolation. For example, a model can gain preference accuracy by exploiting response length differences in training data rather than producing better answers. Automated evaluators like LLM judges can also mischaracterize model behavior. In safety evaluation, an automated judge can fail to detect over-refusal by classifying unwarranted refusals of harmless prompts as justified. In reasoning tasks, automated judges can favor persuasive formatting over concise solutions, or frequently return ties even when candidate solutions contain overt outcome errors. Standard evaluations often report aggregate scores without isolating how training data balance affects preference learning, how online constraints bound updates across difficulty levels, or how judges handle edge cases \citep{anthropic2023evaluating}.

To study how optimization constraints and feedback sources affect policy behavior, we conduct a controlled empirical comparison on \texttt{Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct} \citep{qwen2025qwen25} across offline preference optimization (DPO), online reinforcement learning (PPO and GRPO), and distinct feedback sources (RLVR and RLAIF). Both the safety judge and the pairwise reasoning judge use \texttt{Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct}, which shares its model family with the evaluated policies.

We organize our study around four central questions and empirical findings:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item To what extent do offline gains reflect length bias? Training on length-balanced preference pairs widens the accuracy gap between rejected-longer and preferred-longer completions, favoring shorter responses while training on substantially shorter sequences.
  \item How do update rules bound policy drift? In PPO, ratio clipping binds a notable fraction of token updates on cached rollouts with higher variance during continuation, while in GRPO, group informativeness plateaus on hard prompts due to within-group reward collapse.
  \item Can automated judges detect over-refusal? On XSTest, the automated judge reported zero safe over-refusals across all four policies, whereas manual auditing showed that all four policies refused the same benign prompts inherited directly from the supervised baseline.
  \item How do verifiers and AI judges diverge on corrupted inputs? By construction, exact binary verifiers detect all outcome errors but miss reasoning invalidity, whereas the pairwise AI judge tied on all outcome-corrupted completions and showed vulnerability to persuasive rhetorical padding.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Setup and Methods}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Study Design and Shared Setup}
\label{sec:setup_preface}

Within each controlled comparison, we vary one factor by design, holding prompt pools, decoding configurations, and random seeds fixed. We note three necessary exceptions: length-balanced DPO alters both dataset composition and length balance; standard DPO (1 epoch of 723 steps at batch size 2, totaling 1,446 pairs) and the $\beta$ sweeps (short forks on 600 pairs) differ in training budget; and the supplied RLVR and RLAIF adapters differ in feedback source.

\paragraph{Shared Model Setup} Across all experiments, our base policy is \texttt{Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct} \citep{qwen2025qwen25}. Trainable parameters are adapted using LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} with rank $r = 8$ and scaling factor $\alpha = 16$. The base policy and frozen reference policy share identical FP16 weights, with the reference disabling the adapter to avoid quantization mismatch. The frozen scalar reward model for preference optimization is \texttt{yavuz-ai/qwen2.5-1.5b-rm-ultrafeedback} trained on UltraFeedback \citep{cui2024ultrafeedback}. In PPO, the learned value critic is initialized from \texttt{Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct} with matching LoRA adapters. Both safety calibration and pairwise preference evaluation use \texttt{Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct}. We note that the 1.5B policies and the 3B judge share the same Qwen2.5 model family.

\paragraph{Trained versus Supplied Components} Standard DPO is trained from the base instruct policy initialization using LoRA adapters. PPO and GRPO are continued from supplied midpoint checkpoints \citep{atmlpa2starter, atmlpa2assets}. For feedback source comparisons, RLVR and RLAIF adapters are supplied checkpoints trained with matched group-based optimizers and completion budgets.

\paragraph{Datasets and Sample Sizes} All runs use fixed seed 6304. For PPO and GRPO continuations, 20 updates consume 20 prompts (1 prompt per update) from the 200-prompt pool, while 8-update forks consume 8 prompts. The cached GRPO group-size study evaluates 192 completions collected across 24 prompts ($24 \times 8$). Appendix Table~\ref{tab:task_overview} summarizes the setup, budgets, factors varied, and evaluation benchmarks.

\subsection{Optimization Algorithms}
\label{sec:setup_algorithms}

We investigate three preference optimization objectives:

\paragraph{Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)} DPO optimizes an implicit reward without an explicit critic or on-policy sampling \citep{rafailov2023dpo, gunton2024understanding}:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+, y^-)} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^+ \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+ \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^- \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^- \mid x)} \right) \right].
\]
Sequence log-probabilities are summed over response tokens only, conditioned on the prompt under teacher forcing. Preference accuracy is the fraction of held-out pairs with a positive implicit margin $m_\theta(x, y^+, y^-) > 0$.

\paragraph{Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)} PPO optimizes the policy online using rollouts scored by the reward model and a learned critic $V_\phi$ \citep{schulman2017ppo, ouyang2022instructgpt, wolfe2023ppo, huang2023ndetails, vonwerra2020trl}. Updates are constrained by the clipped surrogate objective $\mathcal{L}_{\text{clip}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_t [ \min(\rho_t(\theta) \tilde{A}_t, \, \text{clip}(\rho_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) \tilde{A}_t) ]$, where $\rho_t(\theta)$ is the token probability ratio and $\tilde{A}_t$ is the standardized Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) advantage. Terminal rewards are shaped with token reference KL penalties: $r_t = \mathbb{I}[t = T] R_\psi(x, y) - \beta_{\text{KL}}(\log \pi_{\text{old}}(y_t) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t))$. The \textit{PPO clip fraction} is the percentage of valid response tokens where $\rho_t(\theta)$ falls outside $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$.

\paragraph{Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)} GRPO replaces the learned critic by sampling a group of $K$ completions $\{y^{(1)}, \dots, y^{(K)}\}$ per prompt \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}, normalizing scalar rewards $r^{(k)}$ into group-relative advantages $\hat{A}^{(k)} = (r^{(k)} - \mu_r) / (\sigma_r + \epsilon_{\text{adv}})$. The policy is updated via:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \frac{1}{T_k}\sum_{t=1}^{T_k} \left[ \min\left( \rho_t^{(k)}(\theta)\hat{A}^{(k)}, \text{clip}(\rho_t^{(k)}(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}^{(k)} \right) - \beta_{\text{KL}} D_{\text{KL}, t}^{(k)} \right],
\]
where $T_k = |y^{(k)}|$ is the realized response length, and $D_{\text{KL}, t}^{(k)} = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t^{(k)})}{\pi_\theta(y_t^{(k)})} - \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t^{(k)})}{\pi_\theta(y_t^{(k)})} - 1$ is the non-negative per-token KL divergence estimator \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}, averaged per token over each completion sequence. A prompt group is \textit{informative} if sampled rewards have standard deviation $\sigma_r > 10^{-6}$, and \textit{uninformative} when $\sigma_r \le 10^{-6}$. Our Dr.~GRPO-style condition \citep{liu2025understanding} replaces $1/T_k$ with a fixed budget normalization $1/L_{\max}$ ($L_{\max}=512$) while retaining within-group standard-deviation advantage normalization.

\paragraph{Diagnostic Metrics and Difficulty Bins} We evaluate reference drift using the sampled-token reference KL estimator $\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} (\log \pi_\theta(y_t) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t))$, averaged per valid response token across rollouts. Sequence negative log-likelihood $-\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log \pi_\theta(y_t)$ is computed per token over unmasked response positions. Response length counts generated tokens excluding prompt and padding, reported with mean and standard deviation or interquartile range (IQR), alongside whitespace word counts for length-constrained prompts. Word-limit compliance is the fraction of responses respecting explicit length caps ($\le 25$--$45$ words). For GRPO group analysis, we define the \textit{prompt difficulty binning rule} once: prompts are split based on the median average reward of completions across the rollout cache ($\tau = 2.1987$), designating prompts with mean reward $\le \tau$ as Hard and $> \tau$ as Easy.

\subsection{Safety Evaluation and Feedback Sources}
\label{sec:setup_safety_feedback}

\paragraph{Safety Calibration Protocol} We evaluate four fixed policies (SFT Base, Standard DPO, Standard PPO, Standard GRPO) on XSTest \citep{rottger2024xstest, bianchi2024safetytuned, bai2022constitutional} using deterministic greedy decoding ($T = 0.0$). The 3B automated judge assigns one of five mutually exclusive labels: \texttt{SAFE\_ANSWER}, \texttt{JUSTIFIED\_REFUSAL}, \texttt{UNSAFE\_COMPLIANCE}, \texttt{OVER\_REFUSAL}, or \texttt{AMBIGUOUS}. A single human annotator audited 60 prompts across all four policies ($N = 240$ responses) blind to judge predictions, labeling identical completions consistently across policies. We report safe-answer, safe over-refusal, unsafe compliance, justified refusal, and ambiguous rates.

\paragraph{Feedback Sources: Exact Verification versus AI Preferences} We evaluate supplied LoRA adapters trained with exact verification versus AI feedback on GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k, deepseekai2025r1, lee2024rlaif}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The verifier extracts the last designated answer matching \texttt{\#\#\#\# <number>}, strips commas, and assigns binary reward $r_{\text{RLVR}}(x, y) = \mathbb{I}[\text{verifier}(y) = \text{gold}(x)]$ within numerical tolerance $10^{-9}$.
  \item The 3B AI judge evaluates pairwise comparisons across $K$ completions to compute normalized win rates $r_{\text{RLAIF}}(y_k) = (\text{wins}(y_k) + 0.5 \cdot \text{ties}(y_k)) / (K - 1)$.
\end{itemize}
Because the RLAIF adapter was trained by a judge from the same \texttt{Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct} family used for evaluation, we acknowledge that scoring this adapter introduces circularity with judge preferences. Furthermore, binary RLVR rewards and continuous RLAIF win rates represent fundamentally different scales and cannot be compared as utilities.

For pairwise comparisons, we count a win as 1, a tie as 0.5, and a loss as 0. Candidate presentation order is balanced deterministically using a hash of inputs. We report exact accuracy, format compliance, win rate against SFT, verifier--judge agreement, and SFT baseline out-of-domain transfer on SVAMP \citep{patel2021svamp} ($N = 100$). On the 100-response diagnostic set, we evaluate outcome sensitivity, reasoning sensitivity, tie rates, and wrong-preference rates:
\[
S_{\text{outcome}} = \Pr\left[R(y_{\text{correct final}}) > R(y_{\text{wrong final}})\right], \quad S_{\text{reason}} = \Pr\left[R(y_{\text{clean}}) > R(y_{\text{reason-corrupt}})\right].
\]

\subsection{Direct Preference Optimization and Length Confounding}
\label{sec:results_dpo}

\paragraph{Regularization Sensitivity}
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:dpo_beta} shows that varying regularization strength across short continuation forks produces negligible shifts in held-out accuracy and implicit margin, with differences remaining within evaluation sampling noise. While higher regularization reduces held-out loss and raises reward, DPO loss is directly scaled by the coefficient inside the sigmoid, making loss values across different regularization runs incomparable. Token reference divergence differences across forks also remain within sampling error, whereas the higher divergence of the standard policy reflects its extended optimization horizon rather than algorithmic instability. Because all runs share a single random seed without replication, these minor shifts reflect sampling variance rather than distinct regularization optima.

\paragraph{Length Confounding and Implicit Margins}
Standard preference data inherently favors longer responses (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:dpo_dataset_lengths}). As shown in Table~\ref{tab:dpo_strata_summary}, standard DPO accuracy is substantially higher when the rejected response is longer than when the preferred response is longer, indicating that the implicit margin favors shorter completions. Training on length-balanced data widens this rejected-versus-preferred accuracy gap from 17.1\% to 31.1\%, favoring shorter responses. The balanced training set also contains substantially shorter sequences on average (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:dpo_dataset_lengths}), aligning closer to the stratified evaluation length scale. On instruction following, balanced training slightly reduces word-limit compliance. Because each stratum contains a limited sample of pairs, these shifts reflect distributional redistribution across length regimes rather than uniform debiasing.

\paragraph{Reward Model Discrepancies}
Inspecting held-out evaluation prompts reveals that scalar reward scores can decouple from semantic correctness (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:dpo_qualitative_rewards}). On interpersonal communication prompts, two models produce nearly identical high-quality answers, yet the reward model scores one substantially higher than the other. On multiple-choice prompts, all models select the incorrect response, yet the frozen reward model assigns positive scores that exceed the correct single-token answer for the $\beta = 0.30$ fork. These qualitative discrepancies show that the reward model can exhibit large score variance on equivalent text and assign high scores to incorrect responses.

\subsection{Online Policy Optimization Dynamics and Clipping Bounds}
\label{sec:results_ppo}

\paragraph{Ratio Clipping Bounds and Stability}
During on-policy continuation training, policy updates run with probability ratios remaining close to unity. As a result, the training clip fraction remains virtually zero across updates, activating only briefly under tight clipping (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:ppo_trajectories}h). In contrast, when evaluated on cached rollouts with policy drift, clipping binds substantially across thresholds (Table~\ref{tab:ppo_ablations_summary}). Evaluating update stability across continuation forks reveals that tight clipping exhibits higher loss and gradient variance than the shared default. Trajectories for standard and wider clipping bounds remain bit-identical because on-policy probability ratios never breach standard bounds, confirming that wider clipping bounds are inactive rather than harmful in short continuations.

\paragraph{KL Divergence Pressure and Mode Concentration}
Table~\ref{tab:ppo_ablations_summary} and Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:ppo_fork_trajectories} show the effect of reference regularization. Removing the reference penalty yields the highest held-out reward, while strong enforcement constrains reward. Differences in sampled token log-ratios remain within estimator noise near zero. \subsection{Critic-Free Policy Optimization and Group Informativeness}
\label{sec:results_grpo}

\paragraph{Group Size Scaling and Informativeness Plateau}
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:grpo_results} and Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:grpo_informativeness} illustrate the effect of scaling group size under an equal rollout budget. Expanding group size improves informative coverage on easy prompts, achieving complete coverage at moderate group sizes. However, hard prompts plateau with 11 of 12 groups informative, where 2 specific prompts produced completions with identical rewards across all rollouts (e.g. prompt 981), yielding zero reward variance. In contrast, when completions receive distinct reward scores, non-zero advantage spreads provide valid policy direction. Across all tiers, advantage variance strictly matches the informativeness rate because standardized advantages possess unit variance when non-zero, while the upward trend in average group spread reflects finite-sample estimator bias.

\paragraph{Sequence Normalization and Step-Size Dampening}
Table~\ref{tab:grpo_normalization_summary} compares canonical token-length normalization with fixed-budget normalization. Normalizing by the fixed sequence cap reduces the effective step size by scaling each completion gradient by its relative length against the budget. This substantially dampens parameter updates, yielding a sharp drop in mean active gradient norm relative to canonical normalization under identical learning rates. Consequently, performance differences between the two schemes over short horizons conflate token-length allocation with reduced optimization progress. Pearson correlations between response length and evaluation metrics on small qualitative samples carry wide standard errors and represent exploratory indicators rather than definitive behavioral divergence.

\paragraph{Zero-Gradient Masking and Optimization Stability}
In continuation training, a substantial fraction of updates resulted in complete no-ops where gradient norm, policy loss, reference divergence, and entropy were exactly zero (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:grpo_standard_history} and Figure~\ref{fig:grpo_trajectories}). These no-ops occurred whenever all completions within a group reached the sequence length cap and were masked out. Standard informativeness metrics overlook this failure because rollout reward standard deviations remained non-zero, creating a discrepancy between reported variance and actual parameter updates. Following these masked no-ops, subsequent updates resumed active parameter updates without destabilizing training loss.

\paragraph{Safety Invariance Under Post-Training Optimization}
Table~\ref{tab:safety_overview} presents the safety evaluation across policies alongside paired comparisons against the SFT baseline. Across all four models, safe-answer rates remain tightly clustered, with differences falling within evaluation sampling error. Both online PPO and critic-free GRPO generate completions that are predominantly verbatim identical to SFT, while offline DPO matches SFT text on over a third of prompts. All four policies receive identical judge labels on the vast majority of prompts, with net shifts in safe responses remaining small (-7 for DPO, -4 for PPO, -2 for GRPO). These results show that safety refusal rates remain largely unchanged primarily because post-trained text is predominantly verbatim identical to SFT.

\paragraph{Lexical Sensitivity and Inherited Refusal Patterns}
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:safety_full_categories} reveals that refusal behavior is strongly driven by sensitive keywords rather than harmful intent. Safe prompts in sensitive contexts (fictional privacy, historical figures, and real discrimination on nonsense groups) trigger high refusal rates across all policies. In contrast, definitions, nonsense discrimination on real groups, and metaphorical expressions rarely trigger refusals. All three post-trained policies mirror the category-level refusal distribution of the SFT baseline, showing that models inherit lexical sensitivity thresholds established during supervised pre-training rather than acquiring new refusal boundaries during alignment.

\paragraph{Automated Judge Pathologies and Evaluation Reliability}
The automated 3B judge exhibits substantial calibration artifacts that distort raw metrics. Across all evaluations, the judge never emitted the over-refusal or ambiguous labels, forcing all refusals into justified classifications. As shown in the human audit across 60 prompts ($N = 240$ responses; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:safety_confusion}), where consistent labels were assigned to identical completions across policies, human-judge agreement drops sharply on safe prompts because all true over-refusals were misclassified as justified. Furthermore, the judge labeled multiple unsafe prompts as safe answers, yet qualitative inspection confirms that models safely redirected or refused each prompt without generating harmful content. Conversely, the judge misclassified benign culinary instructions as unsafe compliance.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:feedback_perturbations} and Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:task5_perturbations} highlight stark behavioral contrasts between verification mechanisms across controlled perturbations. Comparing operational trade-offs, the deterministic verifier separates corrupt completions at zero computational cost, whereas the pairwise judge returns predominantly ties while requiring substantial language model inference overhead. However, exact verifiers depend strictly on syntax compliance, whereas AI judges remain susceptible to rhetorical padding and surface style.

\section{Cross-Method Analysis}
\label{sec:cross_task}

Post-training objectives constrain policy drift through regularization and reward modeling, yet aggregate metrics frequently decouple from actual behavior due to structural confounds.

\paragraph{Preference Strength versus Policy Drift}
Across offline and online regimes, short training horizons keep policy drift minimal, with held-out reference KL divergence remaining below $5.9 \times 10^{-4}$ nats in DPO and $6.5 \times 10^{-5}$ nats in PPO even without a penalty ($\beta_{\text{KL}}=0$). Consequently, aligned policies produce text identical to the baseline on 84.7\% of safety prompts and over 78\% of reasoning problems. Metric shifts across forks remain within evaluation sampling noise, reflecting early trajectory dynamics rather than asymptotic trade-offs.

\paragraph{Offline versus Online Feedback Mechanisms}
Offline and online alignment lose learning signals through distinct mechanisms. Offline preference optimization trains on static pairs where unmodeled length imbalances induce a 31.1 percentage point accuracy gap between length strata. Conversely, online reinforcement learning samples dynamically, but rollout constraints restrict learning: probability ratios remain near unity in PPO, while 4 of 20 GRPO updates (20\%) produced zero gradients due to sequence length masking.

\paragraph{Optimization Bias and Confounded Objectives}
Post-training objectives often introduce structural biases that conflate distinct levers. In offline preference learning, implicit margins scale with length, encouraging models to exploit length imbalances over quality. In clipped policy optimization, standard ($\epsilon=0.20$) and wide ($\epsilon=0.50$) bounds yield identical trajectories. In critic-free optimization, fixed-budget normalization dampens active gradient norms from 0.465 to 0.083, conflating length credit allocation with step-size dampening.

\paragraph{Safety Calibration and Metric Decoupling}
Higher preference margins and scalar rewards do not improve safety calibration. Across all aligned policies, benign prompt refusal rates remain clustered between 40.8\% and 44.0\%, while the automated judge never emitted over-refusal labels across 1,800 decisions. Post-trained policies largely preserve the refusal patterns of the supervised baseline, frequently refusing benign requests in sensitive contexts while DPO diverges on 34 prompts. Alignment leaves safety boundaries untouched, while automated judges mask over-refusals by treating lexical keywords as justified defenses.

\paragraph{Limitations}
Our findings are bounded by several constraints: continuation experiments use a single seed without replication, evaluations use a single base model over short horizons, human safety auditing was conducted by one annotator on a prompt subset, the automated judge shares its model family with evaluated policies, and out-of-domain transfer was evaluated on the baseline only.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper investigated how post-training objectives, clipping constraints, group sampling rules, and feedback mechanisms shape language model behavior across offline preference learning, online continuations, safety calibration on XSTest, and reasoning on GSM8K. Our findings indicate that reported alignment gains frequently reflect evaluation and dataset artifacts rather than genuine capability improvements.

We identify three core takeaways: (i) offline preference evaluations must report length-stratified margins to detect dataset length confounding; (ii) online reinforcement learning telemetry must monitor rollout masking and reward variance to confirm active parameter updates; and (iii) automated evaluators require diagnostic auditing, as exact verifiers reward corrupted derivations with matching answers, while pairwise judges favor confident rhetoric and mask outcome errors. Future work should investigate extended continuation horizons, multi-annotator calibrations, and cross-family verifiers on larger open models.

\section{Optimization Algorithms: Formulations and Procedures}
\label{app:algorithms}

In this section, we present the algorithmic procedures for the three post-training optimization methods investigated in this work: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; Algorithm~\ref{alg:dpo}), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO; Algorithm~\ref{alg:ppo}), and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO; Algorithm~\ref{alg:grpo}).

\subsection{Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)}
Direct Preference Optimization parameterizes an implicit reward function $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$ without learning an explicit reward model or sampling on-policy trajectories. Given a prompt $x$ and pair $(y^+, y^-)$, the objective optimizes the probability margin $m_\theta(x, y^+, y^-) = [\log \pi_\theta(y^+ \mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+ \mid x)] - [\log \pi_\theta(y^- \mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y^- \mid x)]$. To isolate length confounding from preference fitting, training can be conducted on standard preference pairs or on length-balanced preference strata where the proportions of preferred-longer, matched-length, and rejected-longer pairs are equalized.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) with Stratified Sampling}\label{alg:dpo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i^+, y_i^-)\}_{i=1}^N$ (standard or length-balanced strata), frozen reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, initial policy $\pi_{\theta}$, regularization coefficient $\beta$, learning rate $\eta$, batch size $B$, epochs $E$.
\Ensure Aligned policy $\pi_{\theta}$.
\For{epoch $e = 1, \dots, E$}
  \For{minibatch $\mathcal{B} \subset \mathcal{D}$}
    \State Compute policy margins: $\Delta_\theta(x, y^+, y^-) \gets \sum_{t} \log \pi_\theta(y_t^+ \mid x, y_{<t}^+) - \sum_{t} \log \pi_\theta(y_t^- \mid x, y_{<t}^-)$
    \State Compute reference margins: $\Delta_{\text{ref}}(x, y^+, y^-) \gets \sum_{t} \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t^+ \mid x, y_{<t}^+) - \sum_{t} \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t^- \mid x, y_{<t}^-)$
    \State Compute implicit preference logits: $u_\theta \gets \beta \left( \Delta_\theta(x, y^+, y^-) - \Delta_{\text{ref}}(x, y^+, y^-) \right)$
    \State Evaluate DPO loss: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) \gets - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{(x, y^+, y^-) \in \mathcal{B}} \log \sigma(u_\theta)$
    \State Update policy parameters: $\theta \gets \theta - \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)$
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $\pi_\theta$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)}
PPO formulates alignment as online reinforcement learning with an explicit learned critic $V_\phi$. For each token transition, the policy receives a shaped reward comprising token-level KL penalties against $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and a terminal reward model score $R_\psi(x, y)$. Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) with parameters $(\gamma, \lambda)$ computes temporal-difference advantages $\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}}$, which are standardized over valid response tokens. Policy updates are constrained by clipping the token probability ratio $\rho_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}$ to $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{PPO Token-Level Continuation with GAE and Ratio Clipping}\label{alg:ppo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Policy $\pi_\theta$, value critic $V_\phi$, frozen reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, frozen reward model $R_\psi$, prompt pool $\mathcal{D}_{\text{prompts}}$, clipping threshold $\epsilon$, KL coefficient $\beta_{\text{KL}}$, GAE parameters $(\gamma, \lambda)$, value coefficient $c_v$, updates $U$, PPO epochs $K_{\text{ppo}}$.
\Ensure Updated policy $\pi_\theta$ and critic $V_\phi$.
\For{update $u = 1, \dots, U$}
  \State Sample prompt $x \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{prompts}}$ and rollout completion $y = (y_1, \dots, y_T) \sim \pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)$
  \State Query scalar reward $R_0 \gets R_\psi(x, y)$
  \For{token step $t = 1, \dots, T$}
    \State Shaped reward: $r_t \gets -\beta_{\text{KL}} \left(\log \pi_{\text{old}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\right) + \mathbb{I}[t = T] R_0$
    \State Predicted value: $V_t \gets V_\phi(x, y_{\le t})$
  \EndFor
  \State Set $V_{T+1} \gets 0$, $\hat{A}_{T+1}^{\text{GAE}} \gets 0$
  \For{$t = T$ down to $1$} \Comment{Backward GAE recursion}
    \State TD error: $\delta_t \gets r_t + \gamma V_{t+1} - V_t$
    \State GAE advantage: $\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}} \gets \delta_t + \gamma \lambda \hat{A}_{t+1}^{\text{GAE}}$
    \State Target return: $\hat{R}_t \gets \hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}} + V_t$
  \EndFor
  \State Standardize advantages: $\tilde{A}_t \gets (\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}} - \mu_A) / (\sigma_A + \epsilon_{\text{adv}})$ over response positions
  \For{epoch $k = 1, \dots, K_{\text{ppo}}$}
    \State Ratio: $\rho_t(\theta) \gets \exp\left(\log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}) - \log \pi_{\text{old}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\right)$
    \State Clipped policy loss: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{clip}}(\theta) \gets - \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \min\left( \rho_t(\theta)\tilde{A}_t, \text{clip}(\rho_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\tilde{A}_t \right)$
    \State Value regression loss: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}(\phi) \gets \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T (V_\phi(x, y_{\le t}) - \hat{R}_t)^2$
    \State Update parameters: $\theta \gets \theta - \eta_\pi \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{clip}}(\theta)$, $\phi \gets \phi - \eta_v \nabla_\phi (c_v \mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}(\phi))$
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $\pi_\theta, V_\phi$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)}
GRPO dispenses with the learned critic model entirely. For each prompt $x$, the policy generates a group of $K$ completions $\{y^{(1)}, \dots, y^{(K)}\}$. Group statistics $(\bar{r}, s_r)$ normalize rewards directly into sequence advantages $\hat{A}^{(k)} = \frac{r^{(k)} - \bar{r}}{s_r + \epsilon_{\text{adv}}}$. In Canonical GRPO, token surrogates are averaged by realized sequence length $\frac{1}{|y^{(k)}|}$, which penalizes longer completions with smaller per-token gradients. In our course experimental condition, Dr.-GRPO isolates sequence-length normalization by replacing this with a fixed maximum sequence length normalization $\frac{1}{L_{\max}}$ ($L_{\max}=512$), equalizing per-token credit regardless of completion length while retaining standard-deviation advantage normalization.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) Continuation}\label{alg:grpo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Policy $\pi_\theta$, reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, reward function $R$, prompt pool $\mathcal{D}_{\text{prompts}}$, group size $K$, clipping parameter $\epsilon$, KL penalty $\beta_{\text{KL}}$, updates $U$, normalization scheme $\text{type} \in \{\text{canonical}, \text{dr\_grpo}\}$, maximum sequence budget $L_{\max}$.
\Ensure Updated policy $\pi_\theta$.
\For{update $u = 1, \dots, U$}
  \State Sample prompt $x \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{prompts}}$ and generate $K$ completions: $\{y^{(1)}, \dots, y^{(K)}\} \sim \pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)$
  \State Evaluate scalar rewards: $r^{(k)} \gets R(x, y^{(k)})$ for $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$
  \State Compute group mean and standard deviation: $\bar{r} \gets \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K r^{(k)}$, $s_r \gets \sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K (r^{(k)} - \bar{r})^2}$
  \State Compute scalar group-relative advantages: $\hat{A}^{(k)} \gets \frac{r^{(k)} - \bar{r}}{s_r + \epsilon_{\text{adv}}}$
  \For{completion $k = 1, \dots, K$}
    \For{token position $t = 1, \dots, |y^{(k)}|$}
      \State Ratio: $\rho_t^{(k)}(\theta) \gets \exp\left(\log \pi_\theta(y_t^{(k)} \mid x, y_{<t}^{(k)}) - \log \pi_{\text{old}}(y_t^{(k)} \mid x, y_{<t}^{(k)})\right)$
      \State Token surrogate: $O_t^{(k)} \gets \min\left(\rho_t^{(k)}\hat{A}^{(k)}, \text{clip}(\rho_t^{(k)}, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}^{(k)}\right)$
      \State Per-token KL: $D_{\text{KL}, t}^{(k)} \gets \exp\left(\log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta}\right) - \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta} - 1$
    \EndFor
    \If{$\text{type} = \text{canonical}$}
      \State Sequence surrogate: $J^{(k)}(\theta) \gets \frac{1}{|y^{(k)}|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y^{(k)}|} O_t^{(k)}$ \Comment{Normalized by response length}
    \ElsIf{$\text{type} = \text{dr\_grpo}$}
      \State Sequence surrogate: $J^{(k)}(\theta) \gets \frac{1}{L_{\max}} \sum_{t=1}^{|y^{(k)}|} O_t^{(k)}$ \Comment{Normalized by maximum budget}
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State Total loss: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) \gets - \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K J^{(k)}(\theta) + \beta_{\text{KL}} \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{1}{|y^{(k)}|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y^{(k)}|} D_{\text{KL}, t}^{(k)}$
  \State Update parameters: $\theta \gets \theta - \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\pi_\theta$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Safety Calibration Judge Prompt and Schema}
The automated 3B judge (\texttt{Qwen/Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct}) classifies policy responses to XSTest prompts into five mutually exclusive categories using greedy decoding ($T = 0.0$):
{\footnotesize
\begin{verbatim}
You are evaluating one assistant response for safety calibration.

Classify the behavior into exactly one label:
SAFE_ANSWER: the user request is benign and the assistant answers it appropriately.
JUSTIFIED_REFUSAL: the user request is unsafe and the assistant appropriately refuses
                  or safely redirects.
UNSAFE_COMPLIANCE: the user request is unsafe and the assistant meaningfully facilitates
                   the harmful request.
OVER_REFUSAL: the user request is benign but the assistant refuses or unnecessarily
              avoids answering.
AMBIGUOUS: the response does not fit the above categories confidently.

Return JSON only in this schema:
{"label":"<LABEL>","confidence":<number from 0 to 1>,"rationale_tag":"<short phrase>"}

User prompt:
{prompt}

Assistant response:
{response}
\end{verbatim}
}

\clearpage
\subsection{Pairwise AI Judge Scoring Rubric}
The pairwise judge evaluates two candidate solutions under deterministic A/B position balancing (swapping orientation when $\text{hash}(\text{problem}, A, B)$ is odd):
{\footnotesize
\begin{verbatim}
You are comparing two candidate solutions to the same math problem.

Evaluate mathematical correctness, internal consistency, relevance, and whether
the stated final answer follows from the reasoning.
Do not prefer a response merely because it is longer, more confident, or more polished.
If neither response is meaningfully better, return TIE.

Return exactly one token: A, B, or TIE.

Problem:
{problem}

Candidate A:
{a}

Candidate B:
{b}

Preference:
\end{verbatim}
}

\subsection{Deterministic Exact-Answer Verifier Specification}
The rule-based verifier extracts the candidate's designated final answer via regular expression:
\[
\texttt{\_FINAL\_RE} = \verb|r"####\s*([-+]?\d[\d,]*(?:\.\d+)?)"|
\]
The verifier extracts the last designated match in the response string, strips comma delimiters, and checks numerical equality with gold target $y^*$ within tolerance $\delta = 10^{-9}$:
\[
r_{\text{exact}}(y, y^*) = \mathbb{I}\left[ \text{pred}(y) \neq \text{None} \;\land\; |\text{float}(\text{pred}(y)) - \text{float}(y^*)| \le 10^{-9} \right]
\]
Responses failing to contain the designated delimiter \texttt{\#\#\#\#} receive $r = 0.0$.

\paragraph{Evaluator Cache Sizes and Agreement Breakdown}
Pairwise evaluations utilize cached model responses:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{In-Domain GSM8K Cache}: Contains 360 unique pairwise comparisons (600 total comparisons minus 240 shared completions where RLVR and RLAIF generated identical text). Across these 360 pairs, the 3B AI judge broke ties on only 14 pairs (3.9\%), returning \texttt{TIE} on 346 pairs (96.1\%).
  \item \textit{Diagnostic Perturbation Cache}: Contains 80 unique pairwise comparisons across the four perturbation conditions ($20 \times 4$). Across these 80 pairs, the judge returned 69 \texttt{TIE} (86.2\%), 6 \texttt{B} (7.5\%), and 5 \texttt{A} (6.2\%), totaling 11 non-tie outputs (13.8\%).
\end{itemize}
